Interviews are lost not just by poor answers, but by avoidable mistakes that signal poor judgment or lack of preparation. Here are the ten most common ones — and how to avoid each.

## 1. Not Researching the Company

The most common and most disqualifying mistake. If you can't explain what the company does, what stage it's at, who their customers are, and what excites you about them specifically, you haven't done your homework.

**Fix**: Spend 30 minutes before every interview reading their website, recent news, and a few Glassdoor reviews. Know 2-3 specific things you find genuinely interesting.

## 2. Giving Vague, Story-Free Answers

"I'm a great communicator" is an empty claim. "I led a team of 8 through a rebrand in 6 weeks, presenting updates to the CEO weekly, and we launched on time" is a story.

**Fix**: Use the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every behavioral question. Prepare 5-7 "hero stories" from your career before the interview.

## 3. Badmouthing Previous Employers

Even if your last job was terrible, interviewers interpret negativity about past employers as a red flag about your character and professionalism. "What didn't you like about your last role?" is a trap.

**Fix**: Always frame previous experience constructively. "The role evolved away from the work I'm most energized by — I'm looking for more [X]."

## 4. Not Having Questions to Ask

"No, I think we covered everything" is one of the worst things you can say at the end of an interview. It signals low interest and low preparation.

**Fix**: Prepare at least 5 questions. Ask about team dynamics, how success is measured, what the biggest challenge is for the team right now, and what the interviewer personally enjoys about the company.

## 5. Underselling Yourself

Many candidates, especially women and early-career professionals, downplay their contributions. "We did X" instead of "I led X." Humility is a virtue but not in a job interview.

**Fix**: Own your contributions specifically. You can acknowledge your team while still being clear about your individual role.

## 6. Talking Too Much (Or Too Little)

2-3 minute answers are ideal for most questions. Going 5+ minutes per question loses the interviewer. Going under 60 seconds signals shallow thinking.

**Fix**: Practice timing your answers. "Tell me about yourself" should take 60-90 seconds. Behavioral questions 2-3 minutes.

## 7. Not Asking for Clarification

If a question is ambiguous, candidates often guess at what's being asked and answer the wrong question. Interviewers notice when you're not answering what was asked.

**Fix**: It's always okay to say "Just to make sure I'm answering the right thing — are you asking about X?" Good PMs and senior engineers do this instinctively.

## 8. Forgetting to Close

Many candidates leave without expressing clear interest in moving forward. This feels awkward, but it's a differentiator.

**Fix**: Before you leave (or hang up), say: "I want to be direct — after this conversation, I'm even more interested in this role. I'd love to move forward. What are the next steps?"

## 9. Lying or Exaggerating

Interviewers often follow up at reference check stage and with skills assessments. Technical lies are uncovered in the next interview round. It's not worth it.

**Fix**: Be honest about gaps and present them as learning opportunities. "I haven't used [technology] professionally but I've built [project] with it and I pick up new tools quickly — here's an example."

## 10. Skipping Practice

Thinking through answers in your head and speaking them out loud are completely different. Candidates who haven't practiced often ramble, lose their train of thought, or come across as nervous.
