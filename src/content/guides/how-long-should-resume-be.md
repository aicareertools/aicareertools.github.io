A recruiter’s inbox is a crowded, noisy place. Every day they sift through dozens of resumes, each vying for a single glance. If your résumé is too long, it gets skimmed and discarded; if it’s too short, it may feel incomplete. Striking the right balance—so your résumé is concise yet comprehensive—can make the difference between a “maybe” and a “call me” email.

## 1. The One-Page Rule: Why It Still Matters

For most recent graduates and early‑career professionals, a single page is the sweet spot. A one‑page résumé forces you to focus on the most relevant achievements and keeps recruiters’ attention. Think of it as a résumé elevator pitch: you have 30‑60 seconds to showcase why you’re the best fit.

**Actionable tip:** Use a two‑column layout. Put your contact information, headline, and key skills on the left column; use the right column for your experience. This maximizes space while keeping the design clean.

**Example:** Jane Doe, a 26‑year‑old graphic designer, used a one‑page résumé to land a junior designer role at a tech startup. She highlighted two major projects—an app redesign that increased user engagement by 15% and a brand refresh that boosted client referrals by 20%—and omitted older, less relevant positions.

If you’re a recent graduate, aim for one page. If you’re a professional with 5–10 years of experience, a single page is still feasible if you prune older roles and focus on results. The key is relevance, not chronology.

## 2. When Two Pages Make Sense

A two‑page résumé is acceptable—and sometimes necessary—when you have significant, relevant experience that can’t be compressed without losing impact. Senior managers, executives, and candidates with a long, distinguished career often need two pages to fully capture their achievements.

**Actionable tip:** Keep the first page for your most recent, most relevant experience. Reserve the second page for earlier roles that demonstrate breadth and depth but are not central to the position you’re applying for.

**Example:** Mark, a 45‑year‑old IT director with 20 years of experience, used a two‑page résumé for a C‑level role. The first page highlighted his leadership of a $30M digital transformation project, while the second page listed earlier roles that showed his progression from project manager to director, emphasizing skills like stakeholder engagement and budget oversight.

If you’re a mid‑career professional (10–15 years) in a field that values detailed work—like academia, consulting, or engineering—a two‑page résumé can be justified. Just ensure every bullet point on both pages has a measurable outcome.

## 3. Tailoring Length to Industry and Role

Different industries and job levels have different expectations. Knowing these norms helps you decide whether to aim for one or two pages.

| Industry | Typical Length | Why |
|----------|----------------|-----|
| Tech & Startups | 1 page | Fast‑moving, results‑oriented; recruiters prioritize impact over history. |
| Finance & Consulting | 1–2 pages | Showcases analytical skills and project impact; senior roles may need more depth. |
| Academia & Research | 2+ pages | CVs often include publications, grants, and teaching experience. |
| Creative & Media | 1 page | Visual portfolios often accompany the résumé; space is limited. |
| Healthcare | 1–2 pages | Requires certifications and compliance details; senior roles may need more depth. |

**Actionable tip:** Before you write, scan the job posting and similar roles on LinkedIn. Note how many pages the top candidates’ résumés tend to be. That gives you a benchmark.

**Example:** Emily, a senior marketing strategist, applied for a brand manager role at a consumer goods company. She discovered that most brand managers in that sector use two‑page résumés. She kept her first page focused on her current role—leading a 12‑member team that increased brand awareness by 30%—and used the second page to showcase earlier marketing roles, ensuring each bullet highlighted metrics.
