Asking for a raise can feel like stepping onto a tightrope, but with the right data, timing, and script, you can turn the conversation into a win‑win. The key is to treat the request like any other business proposal: come prepared, speak in concrete terms, and align your ask with the company’s goals. Below is a step‑by‑step playbook that takes the guesswork out of the process and gives you a repeatable framework you can use at any stage of your career.

## 1. Build a data‑driven case

### Quantify your impact
Numbers speak louder than anecdotes. Pull together the metrics that demonstrate how you’ve added value:

| Metric | Before you joined | After you joined | % Increase |
|--------|-------------------|------------------|------------|
| Quarterly sales | $1.2 M | $1.8 M | +50% |
| Customer churn | 12% | 7% | –5 pts |
| Project delivery time | 9 weeks | 6 weeks | –33% |

If you’re not in a role with obvious KPIs, translate your work into business outcomes. For example, a software engineer could note that a refactor reduced server costs by $15,000 per month, while a marketer might highlight a campaign that generated $250,000 in pipeline revenue.

### Benchmark your market value
Use the [Salary Estimator](/tools/salary-estimator) to see what peers with similar experience earn in your city and industry. Record the range, then aim for the midpoint or higher—especially if you have a track record of exceeding expectations.

### Document achievements in real time
Create a “raise dossier” in a simple Google Sheet or Notion page. Add a new row each time you complete a notable project, receive positive client feedback, or earn an internal award. When the time comes, you’ll have a ready‑made list rather than scrambling to remember details.

## 2. Choose the right moment and setting

### Ideal timing windows
| Situation | Why it works |
|-----------|--------------|
| End of a successful quarter | Your numbers are fresh and the budget is still being allocated. |
| After a major win (e.g., product launch) | You can directly tie the raise to a high‑visibility achievement. |
| During performance‑review cycles | Companies already expect compensation discussions. |
| When the company announces a profit increase | There’s extra cash on the table. |

Avoid asking during layoffs, budget freezes, or when your manager is juggling multiple crises.

### Secure a dedicated meeting
Send a calendar invite titled “Compensation Review – [Your Name]” and include a brief agenda (e.g., “1) Review FY2024 achievements; 2) Discuss market alignment”). This signals professionalism and gives your manager time to prepare.

## 3. Craft a concise, persuasive script

1. **Open with gratitude** – “I appreciate the support I’ve received on the XYZ project.”
2. **State the ask clearly** – “Based on my contributions and market data, I’d like to discuss adjusting my salary to $95,000.”
3. **Present evidence** – Cite the metrics from your dossier and the benchmark from the Salary Estimator.
4. **Tie to future value** – “With the upcoming rollout of Feature X, I plan to lead the integration, which is projected to increase user retention by 8%.”
5. **Invite dialogue** – “I’m open to hearing your perspective and discussing how we can align on this.”

### Sample dialogue
> **You:** “Thanks for meeting, Sarah. Over the past 12 months I’ve led three product releases that collectively added $1.2 M in ARR and reduced support tickets by 30%. According to the Salary Estimator, the market rate for a senior product manager in our region is $105‑$115 k. I’d like to discuss moving my salary to $110 k to reflect that impact.”
> 
> **Manager:** “Those numbers are impressive. Let me review the budget and get back to you.”
> 
> **You:** “Great, I’ll follow up next week. In the meantime, I’ve prepared a brief summary of the projects and the market data for your reference.”

Keep the conversation under 10 minutes. If the manager needs more time, set a clear follow‑up date.

## 4. Anticipate and address objections

| Common objection | How to respond |
|------------------|----------------|
| “We don’t have budget this quarter.” | “I understand. Could we explore a phased increase or a performance‑based bonus tied to the next product launch?” |
| “Your current salary is already competitive.” | “I appreciate that. The market data shows a 12% gap for comparable roles, and my recent projects have generated $2 M in incremental revenue, which justifies revisiting the baseline.” |
| “We need more evidence of impact.” | “I’ve prepared a one‑page impact summary (see attached). I can also set measurable goals for the next six months to lock in the raise.” |

Practice these rebuttals with a trusted colleague or use the [Interview Questions Generator](/tools/interview-questions-generator) to simulate a tough negotiation scenario.

## 5. Follow up and lock in the agreement

1. **Send a recap email** within 24 hours. Include:
   * Your original ask and the manager’s response.
   * Any agreed‑upon next steps (e.g., “HR will send a revised offer by Friday”).
   * A thank‑you note for their time.

   Example:

   > Subject: Summary – Compensation Review (May 15)  
   > Hi Sarah, thanks for discussing my compensation today. As agreed, we’ll aim for a salary adjustment to $110 k effective July 1, pending HR approval. I’ve attached the impact summary for reference. Please let me know if you need any additional information.

2. **Get it in writing**. Whether it’s an updated offer letter or an email from HR, a written record prevents misunderstandings later.

3. **Plan for future reviews**. If the raise is postponed, negotiate a concrete timeline and measurable targets. For instance: “If we achieve a 10% increase in user engagement by Q4, we’ll revisit the salary at the next review.”
