Salary negotiation is one of the highest-leverage skills in your career. A single conversation can mean $5,000–$20,000 more per year — and that compounds over your entire career. Yet most candidates accept the first offer they're given.

Here's how to negotiate without awkwardness or risking the offer.

## The #1 Rule: Always Negotiate

Unless the offer is truly non-negotiable (rare) or already above market, negotiate. Employers expect it. Making a counter-offer doesn't rescind offers. At worst, they say no — you're no worse off.

## Do Your Research First

Before any negotiation, know your number. Use our [Salary Estimator](/tools/salary-estimator) to get a realistic range for your role, location, and experience. Also check:

- **Glassdoor / Levels.fyi**: Self-reported salaries, useful for ballpark
- **LinkedIn Salary**: Good for broad ranges
- **Bureau of Labor Statistics**: Official data, useful for non-tech roles

Come in with three numbers in mind:
- **Target**: What you actually want
- **Anchor**: What you'll ask for (10-15% above target)
- **Floor**: The minimum you'll accept

## When They Make the Offer

Don't accept or reject immediately. Say:

> "Thank you so much — I'm really excited about this role. I'd like a little time to review the full package. Can I get back to you by [specific day]?"

This is not being difficult. It's professional. Use that time to research and prepare your counter.

## The Counter-Offer Script

When you call back:

> "I've given the offer a lot of thought, and I'm genuinely excited about joining the team. Based on my research into market rates for this role in [location] and given my [X years of experience / specific skill], I was hoping we could get to [anchor number]. Is there flexibility there?"

Then **stop talking**. Silence is your friend. The first person to speak after making a salary request often loses.

## What If They Push Back

If they say the number is firm:

> "I understand. Is there flexibility in other areas — like a signing bonus, remote work, or an earlier performance review?"

Total compensation includes: base salary, bonus, equity, benefits, PTO, remote flexibility, title, and professional development budget. If base is fixed, these are all negotiable.

## What If They Ask Your Current Salary

In many places this is illegal to ask. If they do ask, you can redirect:

> "I'd rather focus on what's right for this role. Based on my research, I'm targeting [range]."

## After You Negotiate

Once you reach an agreement, get it in writing before resigning from your current position. A verbal offer is not an offer.

## Common Negotiation Mistakes

- **Giving a range**: Always give a specific number. If you say "$80,000–$90,000," they hear "$80,000."
- **Justifying with need**: "I need $X because of my rent" is weak. Justify with market value, not personal expenses.
- **Being apologetic**: Don't say "I'm sorry to ask" or "I hate to do this." Confidence signals you know your worth.
- **Accepting on the spot**: Even if you're thrilled, ask for time. You can still accept the same day.

Salary negotiation feels uncomfortable because we're not taught to do it. But it's a professional skill, and employers respect candidates who value themselves appropriately.
