Job descriptions are not honest documents. They're written by HR teams, approved by hiring managers who are too busy to care about every word, and often copy-pasted from old listings. Learning to read them critically is one of the most underrated job search skills.

## The Structure of a Typical Job Description

Most job descriptions follow this pattern:

1. **Company overview** (often generic marketing copy)
2. **Role summary** (what the role is actually for)
3. **Responsibilities** (what you'll actually do — read carefully)
4. **Requirements** (must-haves vs. nice-to-haves)
5. **Benefits** (compensation hints, culture signals)

The richest signal is usually in the **responsibilities** section. This is where you can tell what they actually need done vs. what they're using to attract candidates.

## Decoding "Requirements" vs. "Nice-to-Haves"

A common mistake: treating every requirement as a blocker. Research consistently shows that men apply to jobs meeting ~60% of requirements while women apply only if they meet ~100%. Don't hold yourself to an impossible standard.

**Apply if you meet:**
- All or most of the must-have requirements
- 60%+ of the nice-to-haves

**Must-have signals** (usually labeled or implied):
- "X+ years of experience in..."
- "Must have..."
- "Required:"
- "Bachelor's degree required"

**Nice-to-have signals**:
- "Preferred:"
- "Nice to have:"
- "Familiarity with..."
- "Experience with... is a plus"

## Red Flags in Job Descriptions

Some phrasing patterns signal potential problems:

| Language | What it often means |
|---|---|
| "Wear many hats" | Understaffed, you'll do 3 jobs |
| "Fast-paced environment" | Chaotic, poor planning |
| "Rockstar / ninja / unicorn" | Culture problems, unrealistic expectations |
| "Must be comfortable with ambiguity" | No process, unclear ownership |
| "Unlimited PTO" | Cultural pressure not to take it |
| "Competitive salary" with no range | They're planning to lowball you |

This doesn't mean you should automatically pass on these roles — but they're worth asking about directly in the interview.

## Extracting Keywords for Your Application

The requirements section contains the exact keywords your resume and cover letter need. Hiring managers don't read resumes looking for the best candidate — they scan for signals that you match the role.

## Reading Between the Lines

**"Cross-functional collaboration"** → You'll frequently navigate unclear ownership between teams. Emphasize your communication skills and examples of aligning stakeholders.

**"Ownership mindset"** → They want someone who doesn't need to be managed. Emphasize projects you drove end-to-end.

**"Data-driven"** → Every company claims this. Ask in the interview: "How does the team currently use data to make decisions?" The answer will tell you if it's real.

**"Startup experience preferred"** → They value adaptability and low-overhead working style. Emphasize times you worked without heavy process.

## Salary: Reading the Signals

If there's no salary range in the listing, look for:
- Level in the title (Junior vs. Senior vs. Staff)
- Company size and funding stage
- Location requirements

A startup Series A software engineer in San Francisco without a listed salary is probably $120k-$160k. A VP title at a Fortune 500 in the Midwest is different. Use our [Salary Estimator](/tools/salary-estimator) to benchmark before your first conversation.

## The Most Important Sentence in Any JD

Every job description, no matter how long, has one sentence that captures what they actually need. It's usually in the role summary or the first few bullets of responsibilities. Find that sentence and make sure your application answers it directly.
