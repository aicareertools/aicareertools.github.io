The conventional wisdom is that software engineers don't need cover letters. That's partially true — at big tech companies (FAANG-tier), a cover letter rarely matters. But for smaller companies, startups, and any role where culture fit is a key factor, a well-written cover letter can be the difference between an interview and a rejection.

## When to Write One (and When to Skip It)

**Write a cover letter when:**
- The job posting explicitly asks for one
- You're applying to a startup or small company
- You have an unusual background that needs context (career change, employment gap, bootcamp grad)
- You have a genuine personal connection to the company

**Skip it when:**
- The platform doesn't have a field for it
- You're applying to a large company's ATS portal with hundreds of applicants
- You'd just be writing something generic

## What Makes a SWE Cover Letter Different

Technical cover letters should be more specific than general ones. Hiring managers are technical people — vague enthusiasm doesn't impress them. What does:

- Specific technologies you've used that match their stack
- A concrete project or achievement with metrics
- Genuine familiarity with their product or technical approach

## The Structure That Works

**Paragraph 1 — The hook**: Why this company, specifically. Show you've used their product, know their tech blog, or have a genuine reason beyond "great opportunity."

**Paragraph 2 — Technical credibility**: One or two specific examples of relevant technical work. Include stack, scale, and outcome.

**Paragraph 3 — Close**: What you'd bring to this specific role and a confident call to action.

## Example

> I've been following [Company]'s approach to [specific technical area] since you published your blog post on [topic]. It directly maps to a problem I spent the last year solving at my current role.
>
> At [Current Company], I built a real-time event processing pipeline handling 2M events/day using Kafka and Go. We reduced processing latency from 800ms to under 50ms. I'm comfortable across the full stack but gravitate toward backend systems and data infrastructure — which aligns directly with what you're building.
>
> I'd love to discuss how my experience applies to your current infrastructure challenges. Thanks for considering my application.

## What Not to Include

- Don't explain how you're a "fast learner" — everyone says this, it means nothing
- Don't apologize for missing skills — address them only if directly asked
- Don't go over one page — 3 paragraphs is ideal
- Don't use the same letter for every application — 10 minutes of customization can double your response rate

## Stack Matching Is Critical

Every company has a tech stack. Match your letter to it. If they use Python/Django and you've mostly used Node.js, that's fine — mention your Python experience and your comfort with different stacks. If you have zero Python, acknowledge it briefly and pivot to what makes you a fast learner in new environments (with a specific example).

A technically specific cover letter, even a short one, signals that you're the kind of developer who pays attention to details.
