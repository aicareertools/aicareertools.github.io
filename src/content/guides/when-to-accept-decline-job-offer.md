Landing a job offer feels like a win, but the real test begins when you decide whether to say “yes” or “no.” The choice impacts your salary trajectory, skill development, and day‑to‑day happiness. By treating the offer as a data set—rather than an emotional high—you can make a decision that advances your career rather than derails it.

## 1. Break the Offer Down Piece by Piece  

A raw offer letter can be dense. Translate it into a simple spreadsheet or a checklist so you can compare apples‑to‑apples with your current situation or other opportunities.

**Action steps**  
1. **Copy every component** – base salary, signing bonus, equity, relocation assistance, health benefits, PTO, and any “perks” (gym membership, education stipend).  
2. **Assign a weight** – decide what matters most to you (e.g., 40 % salary, 30 % growth, 20 % work‑life balance, 10 % culture).  
3. **Score each line item** – give a 1‑5 rating against your weight. Add up the weighted scores to see the overall fit.

**Example**  
Maria, a data analyst, received an offer with a $95k base salary, 10 % equity, and unlimited PTO. She weighted salary (40 %), equity (30 %), and PTO (30 %). After scoring, the total weighted score was 78/100, which she deemed “good but not great.” She used the score to negotiate a higher equity grant, pushing the final score to 86.

## 2. Align the Role with Your Long‑Term Career Map  

A tempting salary can mask a dead‑end job. Map the position against your 3‑ and 5‑year goals.

**Action steps**  
1. **Write down your career milestones** (e.g., “lead a product team,” “become a certified AWS Solutions Architect”).  
2. **Identify the skills and experiences the new role promises** (tech stack, leadership exposure, cross‑functional projects).  
3. **Score the role’s contribution** to each milestone on a 0‑5 scale.  
4. **Set a “deal‑breaker” threshold** – if the role scores below 2 for any critical milestone, it’s a red flag.

**Example**  
Ethan, a junior marketer, wants to move into performance marketing within two years. The offer is for a brand‑strategy role with limited data‑driven work. His milestone score is 1/5, so he declines and continues his search for a performance‑focused position.

**Tool tip:** Run the job description through the [Job Description Analyzer](/tools/job-description-analyzer) to surface required skills and compare them with your growth plan.

## 3. Crunch the Numbers: Total Compensation vs. Cost of Living  

Base salary is just one slice of the pie. Include bonuses, equity vesting schedules, and benefits, then weigh them against your personal expenses.

**Action steps**  
1. **Calculate annualized cash** – base + expected bonus + signing bonus spread over a year.  
2. **Estimate the monetary value of benefits** – health premiums, commuter allowance, childcare subsidies.  
3. **Model equity** – use the company’s valuation and vesting timeline to project a realistic cash value (most equity never fully vests).  
4. **Factor in cost of living** – rent, taxes, transportation in the job’s city.  
5. **Compare the net figure** to your current compensation or a target salary.

**Example**  
Lena was offered $120k base in San Francisco with $20k signing bonus and 0.2 % equity. After applying a 30 % tax rate and a $4k monthly rent, her net cash is $78k. The equity, based on a $10B valuation and a 4‑year vesting schedule, translates to roughly $8k per year. The total effective compensation is $86k, well below her $130k target for the area, prompting her to negotiate a higher base or look elsewhere.

**Tool tip:** The [Salary Estimator](/tools/salary-estimator) can factor in local tax rates and cost‑of‑living adjustments to give you a realistic net figure.

## 4. Test the Culture Fit Before You Sign  

Even the perfect salary can’t compensate for a toxic environment. Use the interview process, online research, and informal conversations to gauge cultural alignment.

**Action steps**  
1. **Ask direct questions** in your final interview: “How does the team handle conflict?” or “What does work‑life balance look like here?”  
2. **Check employee reviews** on sites like Glassdoor, but focus on recent comments and patterns rather than isolated anecdotes.  
3. **Request a “day‑in‑the‑life” shadow** or a meet‑and‑greet with future peers.  
4. **Observe communication style** – are emails concise, do managers respond promptly, is there transparency about company performance?

**Example**  
After receiving an offer, Carlos asked to meet two potential teammates for coffee. One described a “always‑on” culture with weekend emails, while the other praised flexible hours and a results‑only work environment. The mixed signals led Carlos to request clarification on remote‑work policy, which the company revised before he accepted.
